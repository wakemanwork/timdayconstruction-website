Fire safety is a critical consideration in every healthcare environment, and veterinary practices are no exception.

From consultation rooms and operating theatres to recovery wards and imaging suites, veterinary clinics must be designed and maintained to protect staff, visitors, and animals in the event of a fire.

Because veterinary facilities often contain specialist equipment, clinical waste, medical gases, electrical systems, and animals unable to evacuate independently, fire safety planning must form an essential part of any veterinary fit out or refurbishment project.

## Why Fire Safety Matters in Veterinary Practices

Veterinary environments present a unique combination of risks and responsibilities. Practices must not only comply with fire safety legislation, but also ensure that evacuation procedures and building layouts account for the welfare of animals, staff, and visitors.

Effective fire safety measures help to protect all life and minimise risk while slowing the spread of fire and smoke. The goal is to maintain safe escape routes, reduce disruption to critical veterinary services and support compliance with UK regulations.

## Fire Doors and Compartmentation

Fire doors play a vital role in preventing the spread of fire and smoke throughout a veterinary practice.

Properly installed fire doors help to:

- Protect escape routes
- Create safe compartments within the building
- Provide additional evacuation time

Areas such as operating theatres, plant rooms, storage spaces, and staff areas may all require specific fire-rated protection depending on the building layout. Regular fire door inspections and maintenance are also essential to ensure continued compliance and performance.

## Fire Detection and Alarm Systems

Veterinary practices should include reliable fire detection and alarm systems appropriate to the building size and occupancy. This can include smoke and heat detectors, audible and visual alarms, zoned alarm systems and emergency lighting systems.

Early detection is particularly important in practices where animals may be housed overnight or within enclosed recovery spaces.

## Fire-Resistant Materials and Finishes

Materials used within veterinary fit outs should support both hygiene and fire safety requirements.

Common fire safety considerations include:

- Fire-rated wall systems and ceilings
- Fire-resistant partitions
- Protected service penetrations
- Certified doors and ironmongery

Specialist areas such as imaging rooms or plant spaces may require additional fire protection measures depending on equipment and building design.

## Evacuation Planning for Animals and Staff

Unlike many commercial buildings, veterinary practices must consider how animals will be evacuated safely during an emergency.

Planning should account for:

- Animal containment and transport
- Clear circulation routes
- Safe assembly points
- Separation of different animal types where necessary

Layout design can significantly improve evacuation efficiency while reducing stress during emergency situations.
